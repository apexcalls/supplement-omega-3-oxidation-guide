Omega-3 Oxidation: How to Tell If Your Fish Oil Has Gone Off

Fish oil is one of the most popular supplements in the world — and one of the most fragile. Its omega-3 fatty acids oxidise readily on contact with heat, light and air, and oxidised fish oil is not merely less effective: some research suggests it may carry compounds you would rather avoid. Knowing how to spot oxidation is essential for anyone taking it.

The signs are sensory. Fresh fish oil has a mild, almost neutral smell. Rancid oil smells sharp, paint-like or strongly fishy; softgels may look cloudy, feel sticky, or leak. The notorious "fish burps" are not normal — they are a symptom of oil that has started to break down. If your fish oil suddenly tastes worse than the last bottle, do not finish it out of frugality.

Storage is the main defence. Buy small bottles you will finish within a couple of months. Refrigerate after opening, even if the label does not demand it. Check the manufacturing date — not just the expiry — because a bottle manufactured two years ago and expiring next year is already well into its decline. And prefer brands that publish oxidation measures (peroxide and anisidine values) or carry third-party certification.

None of this is on the front of the label. The front says "1,000 mg omega-3"; the freshness that decides whether those milligrams do anything is entirely in your hands. That is true of many supplements, but of none more than fish oil.
